## 1. Scale: India does not have enough blood, and Mumbai runs dry every year

**National gap**
- **Benchmark.** WHO's rule of thumb is that donations from at least 1% of a country's population are "generally sufficient" for basic needs, and more developed health systems need more than that [1].
- **The best national estimate.** The 2022 PLoS One study by Mammen et al. (co-authored by NACO officials) put India's clinical demand at **14.61 million whole-blood units a year**. It found supply met **about 93%** of that, which leaves a gap of **"around one million units"** a year [2].
- **Platelets are the weakest component.** The same study found platelet supply met only **77.5%** of demand, against 92.6% for whole blood [2].
- **Replacement donors still fill the gap.** In the same study, voluntary donation is "still around 80%". The rest comes from replacement donation by families, or by professionals posing as replacement donors [2].
- **The official "we're sufficient" claim is disputed.** The government reported **14,601,147 units collected in 2024-25**, which roughly matches the 14.6M requirement on paper. IndiaSpend notes that the requirement comes from a 2018 estimate based on 2017 population figures, and that experts dispute the official 70%-voluntary figure [3].
- **Access is the real gap.** Researchers who analysed e-RaktKosh stock in eight states (46% of India's population) found **0.68 units per 1,000 people within a 60-minute radius**, and only **0.17 in Bihar** [3].
- Global context: high-income countries collect a median of **28.9 donations per 1,000 people**, against 8.5 in lower-middle-income countries [4].

**Maharashtra and Mumbai**
- Maharashtra needs about **4,000–5,000 units a day**, and Mumbai alone uses **500–1,000 units a day** (SBTC figures, May 2026) [5].
- Maharashtra has **410 blood banks, 58 of them in Mumbai** [5]. Each keeps its own inventory, and there is no shared reservation layer across them.
- Stock snapshot from May 2026: **KEM Hospital had 65 units** against a daily need of about 100–129, and **St George Hospital had 4 units** [5].
- **Sion Hospital, 2025:** camps collected **13,872 units**, while **15,052 bags were needed for thalassemia patients alone** [6].
- The shortage is seasonal and predictable. Reports describe repeated summer (April–June) and Diwali shortages as donors leave the city [5].

## 2. The waste paradox: shortage and expiry happen at the same time

- **28 lakh units discarded in five years.** NACO data obtained through an RTI query by Chetan Kothari (Maharashtra) showed that **over 2.8 million units of blood and components were discarded** across India in the five years to 2016-17 [7].
- **6.57 lakh in a single year.** In 2016-17, **over 657,000 units** were discarded, including **over 300,000 units of fresh frozen plasma** [7].
- **Maharashtra led the list.** It ranked among the top states for discarding whole blood and was in the top three for red-cell discards [7].
- The cumulative ~6% wastage came to **over 6 lakh litres**. Media coverage blamed this on a **"lack of coordination between blood banks and hospitals"** [8]. This is the exact gap Blood Bridge targets.
- **Expiry is the main cause.** At a regional centre in Surat (2012–16), **"outdating was the single important cause"** of a 6.60% average discard rate for whole blood and red cells [9]. At a Chennai tertiary hospital (2019–23), the discard rate was **18%**. **Platelets made up 91.6% of discards**, and **97% of discards were for expired shelf life** [10].
- **Why platelets expire.** Standard platelet storage is 20–24 °C with agitation for **up to 5 days**, or 7 days only with extra bacterial testing [11]. A unit that sits unused in the wrong bank for a weekend is gone.
- Note: the "28 lakh" and "6.57 lakh" figures date from 2017. Present them as "the last time the government released discard data", not as current numbers.

## 3. The scramble: families are still made to find blood themselves

- **Policy says hospitals must arrange blood.** The National Blood Policy (2007) says "the practice of replacement donors shall be gradually phased out in a time bound programme to achieve 100% voluntary non-remunerated blood donation". It also makes institutions that prescribe blood "responsible for procurement of blood for their patients" [12].
- **Mumbai's own regulator agrees.** The SBTC director told DNA: "According to the National Blood Policy, hospitals have to arrange for blood." He added that relatives "can't be solely held responsible for it" [13].
- **In practice, about 20% of supply still depends on replacement donation** [2]. A LifeBlood Council founder says hospitals use a loophole to log family donors as "voluntary" [3].
- **Social media became the emergency network.** In 2017 Facebook said blood shortages in India drive **"thousands of people to request blood donors on Facebook each week"** [14]. During 26/11 in 2008, people used Twitter to send "pleas for blood donors" while Mumbai doctors "were faced with low stocks and rising casualties" [15].
- **What e-RaktKosh does and does not do.**
  - It is the government's centralised blood-centre system, and its stock page shows availability by component with a **"last updated" date or LIVE status** [16].
  - IndiaSpend reports that the platform is meant to be live, but **"not all blood banks update it"** [3].
  - Based on the official portal's FAQ and feature description, it has **no one-tap reservation or hold, no bank-to-bank transfer workflow for near-expiry units, and no surge forecasting** [16]. This is our reading of the features, not a published critique.
  - **[UNVERIFIED]** ORF commentary states that "around 22 percent of banks update rarely or not at all". The primary source was not traced, so do not use the number on a slide.

## 4. Doomsday: demand spikes while the supply chain breaks

- **Balasore train crash, Odisha, 2 June 2023.** Officials said **about 500 units were collected overnight** at Balasore district hospital [17]. Generosity was never the problem. The problem was getting the right units to the right hospital: critical patients were moved to Cuttack and other cities.
  - **[UNVERIFIED]** The Hindu reportedly said Balasore's stock rose to 1,700 units in one night, but only 10 units were used there because patients were transferred. The original was not accessible (403). Verify it before using it, because it is the best mismatch story available.
- **26/11 Mumbai, 2008.** Twitter pleas for donors went out while doctors faced low stocks and rising casualties [15].
- **COVID-19 lockdown, Navi Mumbai.** At Tata Memorial's ACTREC centre, **single-donor platelet collections fell from 120 to 69** (about −42%) between the 30 days before lockdown and the lockdown itself, and median daily platelet inventory dropped from 16 to 10 [18]. It is a local case where supply fell while cancer patients still needed platelets.
- **26 July 2005 floods.** The city was paralysed by record rainfall. **[UNVERIFIED for blood]** No source was found that documents blood-bank shortages specifically, so use 26/7 as a scenario label for surge mode, not as a blood statistic.
- **Rare groups are the needle in the haystack.**
  - About **5.8% of Indian donors are Rh-negative** (5.79% across 36,964 donors in Bangalore) [19]. Large Indian studies generally report 4–6%.
  - The **Bombay blood group was discovered in Mumbai in 1952 by Dr Y.M. Bhende**. It occurs in **about 1 in 10,000 people in India**, and up to 0.01% in some parts of Mumbai. People with it **can only receive blood from other Bombay-phenotype donors** [20]. This is the local hook: the rarest group is named after our city.

## 5. Who suffers most

- **Thalassemia.** In 2020 the Union Health Minister said India has the largest number of children with thalassemia major in the world, **"about 1 to 1.5 lakh"**, with **10,000–15,000 born every year** [21]. Children typically need transfusions every 2–4 weeks for life. Sion's 2025 numbers show that thalassemia demand alone can exceed a hospital's entire camp collection [6].
- **Mothers.** **Obstetric haemorrhage caused 47% of maternal deaths** in India (Million Death Study, 2007–14; the share is higher in poorer states) [22].
- **Trauma, the golden hour.** **[UNVERIFIED]** No India-specific statistic was verified on deaths from transfusion delay in trauma. Use the qualitative "minutes matter" framing and the Balasore and 26/11 stories instead.

**Three opening-slide one-liners**

2. "India discarded 28 lakh units of blood in five years, mostly to expiry, while a million units a year go missing."
3. "When Mumbai floods, the blood is out there. Nobody can see it, hold it, or move it in time."
